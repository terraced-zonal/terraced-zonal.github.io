A Linear-Time Construction on 𝕊2 Achieving a Sublinear Energy Gap on Smale’s Seventh Problem

Four published constructions and TerracedZonal (right), with every point's Voronoi cell colored by how far it is from a regular hexagon. The sphere turns slowly; the framed panel below each sphere is an equal-area zoom of the boxed region at the center of view, scaled to unit area per point. Each card also gives the configuration's energy gap per point above the asymptotic optimum1, with the lowest at each N marked.

  1. Energy gap. The logarithmic energy is E = ∑i<j log(1/‖xi − xj‖), with each pair of points counted once (half of the double-counted sum over i ≠ j used in some references). E is dominated by N2 and N log N terms that every well-spread configuration shares, so the cards compare the gap per point (E − EBHS)/N above the Brauchart–Hardin–Saff asymptotic energy EBHS = ½(½ − log 2)N2 − ¼N log N + ½CBHSN, where CBHS = 2 log 2 + ½log ⅔ + 3 log(√π/Γ(⅓)) ≈ −0.0556 is the conjectured optimal constant. Lower is better. Dividing by N makes cards comparable when their N differs slightly (note 5), and a gap per point that keeps shrinking as N grows is a sublinear total gap. The exponent is shaded darker the smaller it is. Below the gap is the energy E itself to 12 significant digits, computed exactly over all N(N − 1)/2 pairs; raw energies are comparable only at equal N.
  2. Perimeter excess P/Phex − 1, where Phex is the perimeter of a regular spherical hexagon with the cell’s area: 0 for a perfect cell and at least 2.4% for any pentagon. Why the perimeter: the Voronoi cells of the triangular lattice are regular hexagons, which have the least perimeter of any partition of the plane into equal areas (Hales’s honeycomb theorem, 2001), and the triangular lattice also minimizes the lattice energies behind the conjectured optimal logarithmic energy (Rankin 1953; Cassels 1959; Montgomery 1988; Sandier–Serfaty 2012; Bétermin–Sandier 2018). It is a geometric diagnostic: no theorem bounds the energy by it, but its mean tracks the energy gap closely across these constructions. Cards report the mean (the energy is a sum over points) and the share of cells more than 1% above Phex (how concentrated the distortion is).
  3. Cell anisotropy √(λmax/λmin) − 1 of the cell’s second-moment tensor measures elongation: it picks up sheared or stretched hexagons, while a nearly regular pentagon or heptagon scores low.
  4. Pentagons / heptagons. Two cells are neighbors if they share an edge, so a cell’s number of neighbors is its number of sides: 6 for a hexagon, 5 for a pentagon, 7 for a heptagon. On any sphere the sum of (6 − sides) over all cells is 12 (Euler’s formula), so when every cell has 5 to 7 sides there are exactly 12 more pentagons than heptagons. Cells with 4 or 8+ sides get darker shades of the two colors.
  5. Admissible N. Not every construction exists at every N: the Fibonacci lattice here takes odd N, and EqAreaIco takes N = 10T + 2 with T = m2 + mn + n2. Each construction uses the admissible N nearest the slider value (shown on its card); ZonalEQ, Hammersley and TerracedZonal use the slider value itself.
